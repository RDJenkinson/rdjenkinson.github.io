## Abstract

Real-time quantum simulation of field theories offers access to scattering and decay dynamics, circumventing the limitations of Euclidean lattice methods. However, representing bosonic fields on qubits requires a finite-dimensional approximation and can entail costly multi-qubit operations. Hybrid qubit-qumode architectures avoid this by encoding bosonic fields directly in oscillators. We formulate scattering in the (1+1)-dimensional Yukawa theory for such a device, with staggered fermions on qubits and the scalar on qumodes. Trapped ions are a natural candidate, with the fermions on the internal states of the ions and the scalar on their collective motion, so that the Yukawa coupling becomes a native spin-dependent displacement. Emulating the device with tensor networks, we study the decay of a single scalar particle and fermion-antifermion scattering around the resonance region, using observables that can be measured on the device natively. We compare the results with those obtained using lattice perturbation theory. The emulation is able to resolve corrections beyond the Dyson summation of the one-loop self-energy.

## Layman's Abstract

Quantum computers promise to simulate the fundamental forces of nature in ways that ordinary computers cannot. One major challenge is representing bosonic fields (like the Higgs field) on a quantum computer, which typically uses qubits that can only be 0 or 1. This paper proposes using a hybrid approach: qubits for the matter particles (fermions) and continuous quantum oscillators called "qumodes" for the bosonic field. This is a natural fit for trapped-ion quantum computers, where the ions' internal states act as qubits and their collective vibrations act as qumodes.

We test this idea on a simplified version of the Yukawa theory, the same kind of interaction responsible for the force between protons and neutrons in atomic nuclei. Using tensor-network methods to emulate what such a quantum device would produce, we successfully simulate a scalar particle decaying into fermion-antifermion pairs and study the scattering of matter and antimatter near a resonance. Our simulations capture quantum corrections that go beyond standard perturbative approximations, demonstrating the potential of hybrid quantum hardware for studying real-time particle physics.
